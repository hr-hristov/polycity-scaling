Polycity's hardware and storage, by city size

Prepared 28 Oct 2026 from Polycity's own runs and result store, 185 city summary files, eight published studies, and Amazon's and Hetzner's price lists read the same day

Executive summary

Six scenarios at once cost $928 a month for a city of half a million and $2,640 for London, simulating 10% of residents, on a 3-year Amazon commitment. Simulating every resident raises that to $1,586 and $7,870 to $13,190. Stored results grow with every scenario kept.

Every scenario runs on 8 cores, Tallinn on 4. Days are for 200 rounds. A month includes the platform. Paris costs more than Madrid because its model holds more streets. Paying Amazon by the hour costs two to three times the 3-year price, and a 3-year commitment is owed whether the machines work or not. Hetzner's largest server holds 512 GB.

What it means

  • Cost follows the city's streets and the share of residents simulated. 10% is the usual choice, and one published test needed 25% to keep every traffic figure true.
  • One scenario is one machine. More hardware runs more scenarios at once, never one sooner.
  • London has never been built in Polycity, so its figures are fitted from 28 measurements of 24 cities.
  • Published models run 300 to 500 rounds. This report plans on 200, which sets the days, not the machine.
  • Stored results grow with every scenario kept. How long they are kept decides the storage bill.

Recommendation

  • Commit to no hardware before the five runs in Tests before buying. Two of them, Paris at 10% and 25%, are already sent to staging.
  • Rent by the hour for the tests. Ten rounds of London at 5% cost about $11 on Amazon.
  • Decide how long results are kept before choosing storage.
  • Choose Hetzner where a scenario fits 512 GB and the hardware is wanted month to month, and Amazon above that.

Polycity has two parts that grow differently. The platform is 18 parts that run all the time, about 32 cores and 87 GB, and grows slowly with the number of planners. The simulated day grows with the city, the share of its residents simulated and the number of rounds. Every figure below is marked measured, fitted or assumed.

Why these assumptions

Polycity's simulated day runs on MATSim, the open transport simulator every study below uses. Numbers in brackets point to Sources.

Polycity's 20 stored runs of 10 to 20 rounds still moved a median 4.2% a round while residents were changing plans [24]. None is shown to have settled.

What follows: this report plans on 200 rounds and prices 500 as the upper count. A scenario started from a settled day is given 50. A Tallinn day at 1% of 500 rounds, about 6 hours, measures the real count.

2. Share of residents simulated

A review of 31 published studies found shares from 1% to 25%, 10% the most common [16]. Its test city kept every main traffic figure at 25% or more, and at 10% some could stray. Buses and trains are harder to scale down than cars.

What follows: 10% is the usual choice, not a proven one. 25% costs more memory and time: for London, 200 GB and 97 minutes a round against 112 GB and 65.

3. Memory per resident and per street piece

Polycity's line, from 27 measured days up to Paris at 5%: 75 KB for each street piece up to 40 GB, plus 50 KB for each resident, plus a quarter and 4 GB for the machine around the engine. Two published runs test it:

Published runSizeMemory reportedPolycity's line
An engine maker's run [22]1 million residents, 250,000 street pieces60 to 90 GB69 GB engine, 90 GB machine
Switzerland at 10% [20]about 1 million residents, 1.5 million street pieces130 GB90 GB engine, 117 GB machine

What follows: Polycity's line falls inside the engine maker's range and 10% below the Swiss figure. The engine maker's rule of about 100 KB a resident gives the upper size, such as 890 GB for London with every resident against 616 GB on the line [22].

4. How round time grows with residents

Across 28 measurements of 24 cities, Polycity's round time grows with residents to the power 0.43: ten times the residents cost two to three times the time. Published runs grow faster at large shares, in step with residents in the Munich study [21].

What follows: the 0.43 power is measured from 1% to 10% only. If time grows in step with residents above 10%, London with every resident takes 655 minutes a round, not 174. Polycity's round is also about 8 times slower than the Swiss model's for the same size [19], which What would shorten a run addresses.

5. Cores a run

For Switzerland at 10%, the engine's traffic replay stopped getting faster past 8 threads [19]. An earlier Zurich study found no gain from more cores [16].

What follows: more cores per scenario buy little speed. Hardware buys more scenarios at once, each on 8 cores.

Hours and memory by city

A run is a fixed start and finish, then rounds. The minutes a round are measured for 28 pairs of city and share of residents, from 24 cities. Fitted to those, a round grows almost one for one with street pieces, the stretches of road the engine moves traffic along, and with residents to the power 0.43. London has no measurement, so London is the fit, with as many street pieces as Paris.

Memory grows with the street network more than with residents. The sizing takes 75 KB for each street piece, stopping at 40 GB, plus 50 KB for each resident simulated. Every reading sits under that line.

What would shorten a run

Hardware sets how many scenarios run at once. Only the software sets how long a planner waits for one, and how much each keeps:

  • Make the traffic replay faster. It is about three quarters of a Tallinn round at 10%. The engine has a faster traffic simulator built for large cities, and whether Polycity's bike share, parking and transit additions run on it is the first test.
  • Carry a day across runs, saving residents' plans every 25 rounds so a run can continue where the last stopped.
  • Settle London's baseline once, and start every scenario from its settled plans for about 50 rounds.
  • Stop keeping the final events file, an exact copy of the last round's, which cuts a run's kept files by about a quarter.
