Polycity's hardware and storage for London

Prepared 28 Oct 2026 from Polycity's own runs and result store, 185 city summary files, eight published studies, and Amazon's and Hetzner's price lists read the same day

Executive summary

One London run needs 8 cores and 112 GB at 10% of residents, 200 GB at 25%, and 616 to 930 GB with every resident. Six full London runs at once cost $7,870 to $29,171 a month on Amazon in London, by machine and way of paying. Hetzner's largest server holds 512 GB, so it carries 10% and 25% but not every resident. Each run also keeps 70 GB of results at 10% and 620 GB with every resident.

What it means

  • No London figure is measured, because London has never been built in Polycity. Its figures come from a fit across 28 measured cities, and 8 in 10 of those sit within 1.74 times of it.
  • No Polycity run has settled yet. Published models of Switzerland and Berlin run 301 and 500 rounds, so 200 is the planning count and 500 the upper count.
  • Rounds change how long a machine is busy, never which machine a run needs.
  • A published test of this engine kept every main traffic figure at 25% of residents or more, and found 10% can stray.
  • The engine is one process, so one run is one machine. Kubernetes places whole runs on servers and never splits one.
  • Storage grows with every run kept. At every resident and 50 rounds, six at once add 18 TB a month. How long results are kept decides the storage bill.
  • A commitment is paid every hour of its term, whether the machine works or not.

Recommendation

  • Commit to no hardware before the five runs in Tests before buying. Two of them, Paris at 10% and 25%, are already sent to staging.
  • Rent by the hour for the London tests. Ten rounds of London at 5% cost about $11 on Amazon.
  • Decide how long a run's results are kept before choosing storage.
  • Choose between Amazon and Hetzner once the tests give London's own memory and hours.

Polycity has two parts that grow differently. The platform is 18 parts that run all the time, about 32 cores and 87 GB, and grows slowly with the number of planners. The simulated day grows with the city, the share of its residents simulated and the number of rounds. Every figure below is marked measured, fitted or assumed.

Storage

Each finished run keeps its files in the file store: the day's events, the residents' plans, noise, exhaust and the maps. Measured in the result store on 28 Oct, a Tallinn day of 10 rounds keeps 1.21 GB at 1% of residents and 4.07 GB at 10%. That is 68.6 KB for each resident simulated, on top of 0.89 GB that does not grow with residents.

Most of it is the day's events, kept three times: after the first round, after the last, and as the final output. For London the part that does not grow with residents is taken as Tallinn's scaled by street pieces: 9.3 GB, assumed. While it runs, a run also needs working disk for its inputs and its files before they are sent, taken as 1.2 times what it keeps.

Why these assumptions

Six assumptions carry every figure in this report. For each one, this section sets what Polycity's own runs show beside what published studies of the same engine found, and says what follows. Polycity's simulated day runs on MATSim, the open transport simulator every study below uses. Numbers in brackets point to Sources.

1. How many rounds a day needs to settle

A day has settled when one more round no longer changes the result. Published runs of this engine:

ModelResidents simulatedRoundsWhat the authors report

Switzerland, the national railway's model, 10% [19]about 1 million30183 hours on an Amazon machine of 32 cores and 256 GB
Berlin and Brandenburg, open model 6.4, 10% [18]10% of the region50045% of residents change plan at first, falling to 1%. Changes stop at 90% of rounds.
Munich [21], summary only1% to 100%five counts testedA later paper reports that smaller shares settled in far fewer rounds, against the Sioux Falls finding

Polycity's own runs: the store still holds the average score of every round for 20 finished runs of 10 to 20 rounds. None passes Polycity's own settling check, which asks the score to move less than 1% over the last tenth of rounds. Over their last three learning rounds the score still rose a median 2.5% a round.

When plan changes stop, at 80% of rounds, the score jumps a median 74% in one round. Every resident then takes their best plan so far, instead of half of them trying a new one. So a short run's last score shows that switch, not a settled day.

Polycity's setting differs from the published ones. Half of all residents try a new plan every round: a new route for a quarter, a new mode for an eighth and new times for an eighth. The other half takes its best plan. The engine's handbook warns that taking the best plan "tends to get stuck with sub-optimal plans" and advises it only beside random picking [17]. Polycity may therefore look settled sooner than it is.

What follows: no published model of a large region stops at 50 rounds. This report plans on 200 and prices 500 as the upper count. A scenario starts from the approved, settled day by default, so it is given 50, which no run has proven. The count changes hours and storage, never the size of a run's machine. A Tallinn day at 1% of 500 rounds settles the question for about 6 hours of rounds, because the Sioux Falls test found the settling round the same at every share.

2. Share of residents simulated

A review of 31 published studies found shares from 1% to 25%, 10% the most common, chosen for the hardware at hand [16]. Its own test city kept every main traffic figure at 25% or more. Between 10% and 25% some figures became unsteady, and at 10% or less they could stray far from the full city. The Munich study found travel times held down to 5% [21]. The review adds that buses and trains are harder to scale down than cars, because they keep fixed routes and timetables. Polycity runs buses, trams and trains.

What follows: 10% is the usual choice, not a proven one. London at 25% is priced beside 10% in the summary: 200 GB a run and 97 minutes a round.

3. Memory per resident and per street piece

Polycity's line, from 27 measured days up to Paris at 5%: 75 KB for each street piece up to 40 GB, plus 50 KB for each resident, plus a quarter and 4 GB for the machine around the engine. Two published runs test it:

The engine maker's rule of thumb is about 100 KB a resident, Java's own overhead included [22]. London with every resident is 616 GB on Polycity's line and about 930 GB on that rule.

What follows: Polycity's line sits inside both published runs, and 10% below the Swiss one. A machine for every resident is sized between 616 and 930 GB, which is why Six full London runs at once says which machines hold each.

4. How round time grows with residents

Across 28 cities, Polycity's round time grows with residents to the power 0.43. Inside one city it is 0.31 for Tallinn from 1% to 10%, and 0.46 for Paris from 1% to 5%. Ten times the residents therefore cost two to three times the time where Polycity measured.

Published runs grow faster at large shares. The Munich study found run time grew in step with residents [21]. The Swiss railway estimated 116 days for 1,000 rounds of all of Switzerland, about 167 minutes a round [20]. That is 19 times its 8¾ minutes of traffic replay at 10%.

What follows: the 0.43 power holds from 1% to 10%, and nothing shows it holds to 100%. If time grows in step with residents from 10%, London with every resident takes 655 minutes a round, not 174. The summary gives both.

A check against the Swiss model: Polycity's fit gives 136 minutes a round for a city of its size. The Swiss model took 16.5 minutes a round on 32 cores [19]. Polycity's round is about 8 times slower for the same size, and the cause is not measured. Item one of What would shorten a London run is where that gap is closed.

5. Cores a run

Polycity gives a large run 8 cores. For Switzerland at 10%, the engine's traffic replay stopped getting faster past 8 threads, on any machine [19]. An earlier Zurich study found no gain from more cores at 100% and 25% [16]. Polycity cut a large city from 8 cores to 6 and lost only 2 to 17%. A spread-out version of the engine is reported 119 times faster, but it is a prototype outside the released engine [23].

What follows: more cores per run buy little speed. Hardware buys more runs at once, each on 8 cores.

6. The start of a run

Each run first reads the city, its residents and its timetables: 3 to 4 minutes for Tallinn and about an hour for a large European city, measured on Polycity. London's start and finish are taken as 2 hours, against 67 minutes measured for Paris. That is under 1% of a 200-round London run, so even double the time moves no total by more than 1%.

Fitted to those, a round grows almost one for one with street pieces, the stretches of road the engine moves traffic along, and with residents to the power 0.43. A measured city keeps its own round, scaled only by its share. London has no measurement, so London is the fit, with as many street pieces as Paris.

What would shorten a London run

Hardware sets how many London scenarios run at once. Only the software sets how long a planner waits for one, and how much each keeps:

  • Make the traffic replay faster. It is 70% of a Tallinn round at 10%. The engine has a faster traffic simulator built for large cities, and whether Polycity's bike share, parking and transit additions run on it is the first test.
  • Carry a day across runs, saving residents' plans every 25 rounds so a run can continue where the last stopped.
  • Settle London's baseline once, and start every scenario from its settled plans for about 50 rounds.
  • Stop keeping the final events file, an exact copy of the last round's, which cuts a run's kept files by about a quarter.
